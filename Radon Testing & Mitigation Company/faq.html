Radon Science & Safety Frequently Asked Questions

Clear, scientifically verified answers to common questions regarding radon radioactive decay, testing protocols, sub-slab depressurization mechanics, and real estate escrow transactions.

Home / FAQ
What exactly is radon gas, and where does it originate in the ground?
Radon (Rn-222) is an inert, radioactive noble gas produced continuously by the natural radioactive decay chain of uranium and radium found inside granite, shale, and sedimentary soils across North America. Because it is chemically non-reactive and has no color, scent, or taste, it migrates easily through soil pores and sub-slab gravel into residential foundations via stack-effect building depressurization.
Why is radon gas dangerous to human health if you cannot smell or feel it?
Radon produces no acute symptoms—no coughing, eye irritation, nausea, or headaches. However, when inhaled, radon decays rapidly into solid radioactive progeny (Polonium-218 and Polonium-214) that adhere to sensitive bronchial tissue. As these particles decay, they emit high-energy alpha radiation that creates double-strand breaks in cellular DNA, making radon the leading environmental cause of lung cancer in non-smokers (over 21,000 U.S. deaths annually per EPA estimates).
What is the EPA Action Level, and why is 4.0 pCi/L the regulatory cutoff?
Under the 1988 Indoor Radon Abatement Act, the EPA established 4.0 pCi/L (picocuries per liter) as the threshold above which remediation is strongly recommended. However, the EPA also advises considering mitigation for levels between 2.0 and 4.0 pCi/L, and the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends an action target of 2.7 pCi/L because there is no known threshold below which radiation exposure carries zero risk.
Can newly constructed, energy-efficient homes still have elevated radon?
Yes. In fact, modern homes engineered with high air-tightness, spray-foam insulation, and tight window envelopes exchange outside air much more slowly than older, draftier properties. When soil gas enters through plumbing slab penetrations or cold joints in an airtight home, the trapped gas accumulates to significantly higher indoor concentrations unless an active or passive soil depressurization system is active.
What is the difference between short-term (48-hour) and long-term testing?
Short-term testing (48 to 96 hours) uses continuous electronic monitors or charcoal canisters under closed-building conditions to capture an immediate, representative baseline—essential for time-sensitive real estate transactions. Long-term testing (90 to 365 days) uses alpha-track detectors to calculate a rolling seasonal average, reflecting year-round variations across changing heating, cooling, and barometric seasons.
What are "Closed-Building Conditions" and why are they mandatory?
ANSI/AARST protocols require all exterior windows and exterior doors to remain closed for at least 12 hours prior to testing and throughout the entire 48-hour measurement window (normal entry and exit is permitted). Whole-house attic fans, window exhaust units, and wood fireplaces must remain off. Normal central heating and air conditioning operation is allowed. This standard ensures a stable, worst-case realistic representation of soil gas ingress.
Where in the building should a radon testing device be positioned?
The device must be placed in the lowest habitable living area of the property (such as a basement family room or first floor in slab-on-grade homes). It must sit at least 20 inches above the floor, at least 3 feet from exterior windows or doors, and away from direct drafts, heating vents, direct sunlight, and high-humidity areas like bathrooms or laundry rooms.
Why do consumer plug-in digital monitors disagree with professional tests?
Consumer smart-home sensors are helpful for broad personal tracking, but they are uncalibrated consumer electronic devices without biennial NIST-traceable chamber certification. Professional continuous radon monitors (such as Sun Nuclear 1028 units) feature precision pulsed-ion chambers, automated temperature and barometric pressure compensation, and internal motion accelerometers to audit against accidental or intentional tampering.
How does an active sub-slab depressurization (ASD) system work?
A small suction cavity is excavated through the concrete basement floor. A 3-inch or 4-inch Schedule 40 PVC vent pipe connects the pit to an exterior or attic-mounted inline centrifugal exhaust fan. The continuous vacuum creates a negative pressure zone beneath the entire concrete slab, drawing soil gases and moisture away before they can enter the living envelope and expelling them safely above the roofline.
Can a home with an unfinished dirt crawlspace be mitigated effectively?
Yes, through Sub-Membrane Depressurization (SMD). Our technicians install a heavy, durable 20-mil cross-laminated polyethylene barrier across the entire dirt floor, sealing all seams with mastic and securing edges to foundation walls with mechanical batten strips. Perforated suction collector piping beneath the membrane connects to an active exhaust fan, capturing soil gas directly under the barrier.
How much electricity does a radon mitigation fan consume per month?
Modern high-efficiency centrifugal radon fans draw between 45 and 85 watts of power—comparable to a single standard incandescent light bulb. In most utility regions, continuous 24/7 fan operation adds approximately $5.00 to $10.00 per month to your residential electric bill.
How long do radon fans last, and how do I verify the system is running?
Quality commercial-grade radon fans typically operate continuously for 7 to 12 years. Every ClearGround system is equipped with an eye-level U-tube liquid manometer mounted on the interior vertical pipe. The differential height between the two fluid columns provides a 24/7 visual proof of active sub-slab vacuum. If the fluid columns become equal, fan service is required.
How quickly can a real estate radon test be performed during an escrow contingency?
ClearGround provides priority 24-hour dispatch for real estate contingencies. The test takes exactly 48 hours to complete. Once our certified technician retrieves the digital monitor, data telemetry is verified and a certified PDF inspection report is issued within 2 to 4 hours—easily fitting inside standard 5-to-10-day inspection windows.
Who typically pays for testing and mitigation in a real estate purchase?
In standard real estate purchase agreements, the homebuyer commissions and pays for the initial 48-hour diagnostic test during the home inspection period. If results meet or exceed the EPA 4.0 pCi/L action level, buyers routinely submit a repair request or credit addendum asking the seller to pay for a certified active mitigation installation prior to settlement.
What happens if a home seller refuses to mitigate elevated radon?
If the current buyer exercises their inspection contingency to cancel the contract, the seller and listing agent are legally required in most jurisdictions to disclose the known high radon reading as a material defect to all subsequent prospective buyers. Because an unmitigated test remains on property disclosure records, most sellers agree to install a system or offer closing credits.
Can closing escrow hold funds in escrow if mitigation cannot finish before closing?
Yes. Title companies and escrow officers routinely draft an "Escrow Holdback Agreement." Typically, 1.5x of the contractor's written estimate is held in escrow from seller proceeds at settlement. ClearGround then installs the system immediately after closing, conducts a 48-hour clearance test, and delivers verification to the escrow officer to release the holdback.
How should I prepare my home before a ClearGround technician arrives?
For testing: ensure all exterior windows and doors have been closed for at least 12 hours prior to arrival. Keep internal room doors open to allow standard air mixing. For mitigation installations: clear a 3-foot workspace around your basement perimeter, furnace area, and sump basin, and ensure our crew has access to your electrical breaker panel.
Does a homeowner need to stay on-site during the 48-hour testing window?
No. Our technician only requires access for 15 minutes during deployment and 15 minutes during retrieval 48 hours later. Normal living, working, and sleeping in the home is completely safe and encouraged. For vacant properties or real estate inspections, technician access via electronic lockbox or keypad code is standard protocol.
How quickly will I receive my certified inspection report?
Continuous digital monitors store hourly telemetry on onboard flash memory. Once picked up, data is uploaded to our central laboratory portal and processed within 2 to 4 hours. You, your realtor, and your closing officer receive an official PDF report complete with hourly barometric charts and EPA compliance certification.
What guarantee does ClearGround provide after a mitigation system is installed?
Every ClearGround active mitigation installation includes a written guarantee that post-mitigation indoor radon levels will measure below 2.0 pCi/L (typically dropping well below 1.2 pCi/L). We verify this through an independent 48-hour clearance test included at no additional charge. Every system includes our 10-year transferable craftsmanship and fan warranty.

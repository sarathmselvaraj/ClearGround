Building Science & Soil Physics

How Radioactive Radon Enters Foundations: An Architectural Breakdown

By ClearGround Technical Editorial • Published: Oct 2, 2026 • 6 Min Read • Standardized Building Science

To most homeowners, a concrete foundation feels like an impenetrable barrier. In reality, standard residential concrete behaves like a hard sponge—riddled with microscopic capillary pores, thermal shrinkage cracks, perimeter expansion seams, and plumbing penetrations that subterranean soil gas readily exploits.

1. The Geological Origin: Uranium Decay to Radon Gas

Radon does not originate in building materials or indoor items. It is a natural radioactive byproduct of the slow decay chain of Uranium-238, an element found naturally in granitic bedrock, shale formations, phosphate rock, and glacial till deposits across the country. As Uranium breaks down, it creates Radium-226, which subsequently transforms into Radon-222 gas.

Because Radon is a chemically unreactive noble gas, it binds to nothing in the soil. It detaches from solid mineral grains into soil water and pore air, migrating toward regions of lowest atmospheric resistance.

"The stack effect turns heated residential buildings into gentle subterranean vacuum pumps, drawing soil gases directly from the sub-grade gravel bed."

— Building Science Principles

2. The Thermal "Stack Effect" Vacuum

Why does radon enter a house rather than dissipating harmlessly into outdoor air? The answer is building physics. Warm air inside a heated house is lighter and less dense than colder outdoor air. As warm air rises toward upper stories and escapes through ceiling fixtures, attic vents, and chimney flues, it creates an area of lower air pressure at the base of the house.

This negative pressure differential—often as small as 4 to 10 Pascals—is more than enough to pull soil gases out of the surrounding aggregate bed through microscopic structural seams.

3. The Primary Entry Pathways

Field diagnostics evaluate six primary infiltration pathways:

  • Perimeter Floor-to-Wall Joints: Concrete slabs shrink slightly as they cure, creating an open hairline gap between the floor slab and foundation wall footer.
  • Unsealed Sump Pump Basins: Open sump pits provide an unobstructed pathway for soil gases to rise directly into basement air.
  • Plumbing and Sewer Penetrations: Open annular spaces around cast iron and PVC drainage lines piercing the concrete slab.
  • Hollow Block Foundation Walls: The hollow cores of concrete masonry units (CMU) channel radon upward from the footing to the sill plate.
  • Dirt Crawlspaces: Exposed soil floors lack any impermeable boundary, venting gas directly into floor joists and living areas.

4. How Active Mitigation Reverses the Physics

You cannot stop the soil from creating radon, nor can you permanently caulk every microscopic concrete pore. The only permanent engineering solution is to reverse the pressure gradient.

By excavating a small collection cavity beneath the slab and connecting it to a continuously operating inline centrifugal exhaust fan, ClearGround establishes a zone of negative pressure beneath the foundation that exceeds the house's stack pressure. Soil gas is collected under the slab and safely routed above the roofline.
